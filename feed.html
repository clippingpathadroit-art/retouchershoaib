The Science of Retaining Natural Skin Texture

Smooth skin is easy. Believable skin is the craft.

Why smoothing backfires

Automated smoothing and heavy blur work by averaging neighbouring pixels. That removes blemishes, but it also removes the pores, fine lines and micro-contrast that make a face read as a face. On a phone screen it can pass. On a billboard, a magazine spread or a 100% zoom, it looks like plastic, and luxury beauty clients notice immediately.

What we do instead

We separate colour from texture with frequency separation and treat each on its own. Blemishes, redness and uneven tone are corrected on the colour layers, while the texture layer stays untouched except where a spot needs to be cleaned by hand. Manual dodge and burn on top shapes light and shadow with small, slow adjustments, so cheekbones, jawlines and highlights are sculpted without painting over the pores.

Where we stop

Retouching should leave the person recognisable. We keep pores, fine lines and natural asymmetry, and we apply the same treatment across a whole campaign so image 1 and image 40 feel like one shoot. Every file is checked at 100% zoom, then again at the size it will be used. Everything stays on separate layers, so changes after client feedback can be refined without starting over.

Mastering Specular Highlights on Diamonds

On a diamond or a polished steel case, the brightest points are not flaws. They are the proof of quality.

Highlights are information

Specular highlights tell the eye that a stone is well cut and that metal is properly polished. The job is to clean around them, never through them. Once a highlight is flattened, the piece stops looking expensive, however clean the surface is.

Working with focus-stacked files

Macro jewelry and watch shots are usually built from many frames so that the whole piece is sharp. Misalignment, ghosting at facet edges and stacking artefacts show up first, so we inspect the stack at 100% before anything else. Seams are repaired from the source frames wherever possible, and only then does cleaning begin, so we are never retouching on top of a problem.

Cleaning without flattening

Dust, micro-scratches and welding marks are removed on separate layers, one area at a time. Gemstones and metal are handled separately: stones keep their sparkle and contrast, while metal gets smooth gradient transitions instead of patchy fixes. On watch dials and crystals we control reflections selectively, so the dial stays readable without looking artificial. Gold and platinum are balanced to the real material under the client reference, not to a preset. The last check is simple: highlights not clipped to pure white, shadows not crushed, facets still defined.

Why Hand-Drawn Pen Tool Outperforms Automated Masks

A cut-out is judged at its edge, and the edge is where automation is weakest.

Edges are where quality shows

Automated selections work well on high-contrast subjects. They struggle with low-contrast edges, thin straps, chain links and transparent fabrics, leaving halos, jagged outlines or lost detail. These flaws are small in a thumbnail and obvious on a zoomed product page.

The right tool for each edge

Hard edges such as watch cases, box corners and garment seams get a hand-drawn pen path, which produces clean, smooth curves that hold up when enlarged. Soft or semi-transparent areas such as lace, tulle and hair are handled with channel-based masks, so the transparency is kept instead of cut away. Automated tools can speed up a rough first selection, but the final edge is always checked and corrected by hand.

Consistency across a catalogue

Marketplaces such as Amazon typically expect a pure white main image (#FFFFFF), and a storefront looks best when every product is cut the same way. Across hundreds of SKUs, hand-drawn paths give edges that look identical from the first image to the last. A cleaner cut also means fewer rounds of revision, and a catalogue that reads as one brand.
